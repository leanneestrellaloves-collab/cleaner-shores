Marine Plastic Pollution Reduction Initiative · EVPC 321 · Leanne Dominguez

Imagine this

A beach day should leave memories—not plastic behind.

Imagine a family arriving at their favorite Texas Gulf Coast beach. Before the children can search for shells, they find bottle caps, food wrappers, and discarded fishing line scattered across the sand. A sea turtle may encounter that same debris long after the family goes home.

For coastal residents, anglers, businesses, and visitors, this is more than an eyesore. It is a shared challenge affecting habitat quality, recreation, and stewardship of the places we love.

Opening scenario is illustrative, not a documented incident.

The problem is persistent—and bigger than a single cleanup.

Plastic fragments, consumer litter, fishing materials, and industrial pellets can accumulate across coastal environments. Currents, storms, tides, and ongoing activity can continually bring debris back. Research supports repeated surveys and trained citizen scientists to better understand these patterns (Wessel et al., 2019; Tunnell et al., 2020; Davies et al., 2022).

Why this matters on the Texas coast

Plastic pollution does not disappear when a cleanup ends.

The northern Gulf of Mexico's barrier islands are subject to debris accumulation that varies across locations and survey periods. Wessel et al. (2019) used systematic shoreline surveys to document those patterns. Gulf-wide citizen-science work by Tunnell et al. (2020) also demonstrates how trained participants can record plastic pellet pollution at multiple sites.

📍

Geographic reach

Our proposal begins with six fixed 100-meter transects across three coastal areas, selected only after access permissions and local consultation. These are proposed monitoring sites, not existing stations.

🔁

Repeated exposure

Waves, storms, tides, and human activity complicate shoreline litter patterns. Monthly measurements can help distinguish a persistent trend from a one-day snapshot (Wessel et al., 2019).

🧪

Research-informed action

Davies et al. (2022) reported citizen-science observations relevant to changes in marine plastic pollution and policy. Our project proposes recording local policy context when choosing comparison sites, without assuming any policy caused observed changes.

Why no headline pollution number? The figures on this website describe proposed project targets, not measured Texas Gulf Coast conditions. We will establish actual local baseline counts during the first project phase.

Our 12-month mission

Reduce visible shoreline plastic—and build the community capacity to keep protecting the coast.

At selected, permission-approved Texas Gulf Coast sites, volunteers and partners will combine standardized monitoring, organized cleanups, education, and prevention.

How we'll know it works

Teams will survey six proposed fixed 100-meter shoreline segments monthly and compare plastic counts at baseline, month 6, and month 12.

Volunteer teams will use structured digital forms (proposed: KoboToolbox), photographic records, and QGIS mapping. Cleanup logs will also track kilograms collected, debris categories, and volunteer-hours. Tide, weather, season, and effort will be recorded to interpret trends responsibly.

Targets are proposed planning benchmarks—not results or guaranteed impacts. A change in debris counts alone cannot prove causation.
Project blueprint & LogFrame

From community action to measurable change

Two coordinated pathways connect practical activities with tangible outputs and long-term outcomes. Each includes indicators, verification methods, risks, and assumptions.

Pathway 01 · Shoreline stewardship

Outcome: Less observed plastic accumulation

Indicators: ≥20% decline in total plastic items/100 m and ≥15% decline in fishing/boating plastic items/100 m by month 12.

Verification: Repeat monthly transects, classified item counts, GPS-enabled forms, Excel charts, and recorded weather/tides.

Risk: Storms and seasonal debris influx may mask improvements.
Assumption: Sites remain accessible and volunteers classify debris consistently.

Output: Reliable monitoring and cleanup dataset

Indicators: ≥12 monthly survey rounds per site, including baseline; ≥90% of accepted records have complete core fields.

Verification: Time-stamped survey exports, GPS locations, photo audits, and monthly reports.

Risk: Missing data and inconsistent sampling.
Assumption: Working devices, quality checks, and safe field conditions.

Activity: Survey and clean up

Indicators: ≥12 documented visits per site; ≥4 organized cleanups with weights and volunteer-hours recorded.

Verification: Field calendars, digital forms, geotagged photos, digital-scale logs, and attendance sheets.

Risk: Heat, sharp debris, storms, and cancellations.
Assumption: PPE, safety briefings, waste disposal, and backup dates.
Pathway 02 · People & prevention

Outcome: Stronger community participation

Indicators: ≥30 trained volunteers; ≥80% valid survey submissions (accepted after completeness and quality review); ≥2 prevention actions operating at month 12.

Verification: Training rosters, submission audits, partner records, installation photos, and 30-/90-day inspections. A valid submission must contain the required date, site, transect length, debris counts, effort, environmental observations, and usable photos, with no unresolved quality-control issues.

Risk: Volunteer turnover and delayed partnerships.
Assumption: Community interest, accessible training, and willing partners.

Output: Education and prevention resources

Indicators: ≥4 training sessions with ≥30 unique attendees; ≥2 partner-approved measures launched, such as fishing-line disposal stations or signs.

Verification: Agendas, attendance records, quizzes, partner sign-offs, photos, and inspection logs.

Risk: Supply costs and approval delays.
Assumption: Venues, materials, and permissions are available.

Activity: Train and coordinate

Indicators: ≥4 sessions, with ≥80% of attendees completing paired pre/post quizzes; ≥3 partner meetings with decisions and owners recorded.

Verification: Attendance and quiz forms, meeting notes, emails, and action trackers.

Risk: Low turnout or weak partner engagement.
Assumption: Outreach reaches participants and partners follow through.

A practical year of action

These phases are proposed and will be adjusted after site permissions, baseline findings, and community consultation. The initiative would seek grants, local sponsorships, and in-kind support; funding and partnerships are not yet confirmed.

Months 1–2

Listen & establish baseline

Confirm partners, sites, permissions, safety procedures, and 100-m transects.

Months 3–5

Mobilize

Train volunteers, run repeat surveys, hold cleanups, and prioritize debris sources.

Months 6–9

Review & prevent

Assess midpoint trends, implement partner-approved measures, and monitor upkeep.

Months 10–12

Measure & sustain

Complete surveys, review results, and share a plan for continued stewardship.
